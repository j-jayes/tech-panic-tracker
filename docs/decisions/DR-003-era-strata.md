# DR-003 — Era strata: confirmed provisionally, with one boundary flagged for revision

**Date:** 2026-08-26 · **Status:** accepted provisionally; the E2/E3 boundary is **open for Ben's decision before Registration 1** · **Closes:** OPEN-QUESTIONS 8 (partially)

## Decision

The five era strata are confirmed for the pilot as **E1 1800–1870 · E2 1870–1920 · E3 1920–1955 · E4 1955–1995 · E5 1995–present**, with the justification below attached to each boundary. Three boundaries are well supported, one is a stated convention, and **one is not supported by anything we could find**.

| Boundary | Status | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| 1800 (start) | **Convention, not a break** | The debate predates it (Steuart 1767, Mortimer 1772). Say so in the paper rather than implying a scholarly break. |
| 1870 | **Well supported** | Mokyr 1999; Gordon 2012. Landes supports ~1870 as a periodization break but for a different reason — see below. |
| 1920 | **Not supported — recommend moving to 1928** | Every independent line of evidence converges on 1928, not 1920. |
| 1955 | **Very strongly supported** | Documentable to the page from the congressional hearings themselves. |
| 1995 | **Supported** | Oliner & Sichel 2000; Rifkin 1995. Not Gordon, whose own break is 1994. |

## Justification by boundary

**1870.** Joel Mokyr, "The Second Industrial Revolution, 1870–1914", in Valerio Castronovo, ed., *Storia dell'economia Mondiale* (Rome: Laterza, 1999), pp. 219–245, opens: "The second Industrial Revolution is usually dated between 1870 and 1914… the rapid rate of pathbreaking inventions (macroinventions) slowed down after 1825, and picked up steam again in the last third of the century." Robert J. Gordon, "Is U.S. Economic Growth Over?", NBER Working Paper 18315 (2012), p. 3: "The second industrial revolution (IR #2) within the years 1870-1900 created within just a few years the inventions that made the biggest difference to date in the standard of living."

**A caution about Landes.** *The Unbound Prometheus* (CUP, 1969) is commonly cited for an 1870 break, and it does support one — but not the one people assume. His own chapter seam is **1873**, and he treats it as the *end* of the first Industrial Revolution ("the evening of the Industrial Revolution", p. 235), dating the second cluster to **the 1890s**. He is also openly sceptical of the "Second Industrial Revolution" label, always attributing it to others ("some writers have come to call", p. 196). The one place he speaks in his own voice about ~1870 as a break is the Preface, p. vii: it is where "the leading industrial nations of continental Europe had effected their own breakthroughs… and were prepared to compete with Britain on even terms". Cite Landes for that, not for an 1870 Second Industrial Revolution.

**1920 — the boundary that does not hold.** We could find no scholarly support for 1920. The evidence converges instead on **1928**:

- The first print appearance of "technological unemployment" is February 1928 — an unsigned *New Republic* article of 8 February 1928, "The Price of Industrial Progress", reprinted into the *Congressional Record* on 21 February 1928 (70th Cong., 1st Sess., p. 3320) at Senator Robert F. Wagner's request. It appears there **in scare quotes**: "the problem of ' technological ' unemployment will be with us permanently." A search of the bound *Congressional Record* back to 1873 returns no earlier hit.
- The Hoover Committee's *Recent Economic Changes in the United States* (NBER, 1929), p. xvii, still calls it "what has come to be known as 'technological' unemployment" and "this newer problem" — scare quotes and novelty, in 1929.
- Katselidis, Vouldis & Michaelides, *International Journal of Social Economics* 38(6) (2011), date "the famous Debate on Technological Unemployment (1928-1933)".
- Amy Sue Bix, *Inventing Ourselves Out of Jobs? America's Debate over Technological Unemployment, 1929–1981* (Johns Hopkins, 2000), starts at 1929 in her subtitle.

**Recommendation for Ben:** move the E2/E3 boundary from 1920 to 1928. This is a substantive research decision for the two PIs, so it is recorded here rather than applied. The cost of moving is one line in the search protocol and a re-run of the era-stratified queries; the cost of not moving is a boundary the paper cannot defend.

**1955.** The strongest break in the scheme, documentable from the primary record. The prehistory runs: Delmar Harder organises an Automation Department at Ford in **April 1947** (Hounshell, *Business and Economic History* 24(1), 1995, p. 70); first print use in *American Machinist*, 21 October 1948; Diebold's *Automation* (Van Nostrand, 1952); then nine days of congressional hearings in October 1955. Subcommittee chair Wright Patman, p. 51 of those hearings, on the word itself: it "does not appear in recent dictionaries although we can scarcely pick up a magazine without finding a reference to it." A word absent from dictionaries and already generating congressional hearings is exactly what an era boundary should look like.

**1995.** Oliner & Sichel, *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 14(4) (2000), p. 3: "From the end of 1995 through the end of 1999, real gross domestic product rose at an annual rate of more than 4 percent." Note that **Gordon's own break is 1994**, not 1995 ("1970–1994, 1994–2004, and 2004–2015"), so cite Oliner & Sichel for a 1995 boundary. Rifkin's *The End of Work* (1995) marks the discourse side of the same moment.

## Known challenges to record in the paper

1. **E2 is a period of popular anxiety and professional silence.** Mokyr, Vickers & Ziebarth (*JEP* 29(3), 2015) cite Woirol (1996, p. 20) that in the late nineteenth century "the employment effects of technological change ceased to be seen as a relevant problem" for the economics profession, even while the congressional record shows sustained public concern (Atack, Margo & Rhode, *JEP* 33(2), 2019). Our thin E2 stratum may reflect a real absence in the professional literature, not only a retrieval failure — which changes how the coverage limitation should be written.
2. **Mokyr, Vickers & Ziebarth do not periodize chronologically at all.** They organise by three kinds of anxiety, run the late eighteenth to early twentieth century as one block, and skip the mid-century automation debate entirely. Our bands are a discourse-retrieval instrument, not a claim about their scheme, and the paper should say so.
3. **Allen's "Engels' pause"** (*Explorations in Economic History* 46(4), 2009) puts the material break of the industrial revolution mid-century, which would cut E1 in half.
4. **Measured labour-market disruption runs opposite to the discourse.** Atkinson & Wu (ITIF, 2017) find occupational churn peaked in the 1960s at 46 percent and fell to 10 percent in the 2010s. If E5 were defined by disruption rather than by discourse, it would not be an era at all.

## Consequences

- The bands stand for the pilot; the search protocol is unchanged for now.
- The 1928 recommendation is on the agenda for Registration 1, and appears in the pilot notebook as an explicit ask.
- The paper's methods section carries the per-boundary justification above, including the Landes caution, rather than an unsourced periodization table.
